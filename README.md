# Investigating the Relationship Between LinkedIn Usage and Impostor Phenomenon
My Bachelor's Thesis (Independent Research Project) investigated the following research questions: 
1. Is there a relationship between LinkedIn usage and impostor phenomenon?
2. Is the relationship between LinkedIn usage and impostor phenomenon mediated by social comparison?

To address these research questions, data on LinkedIn usage, social comparison, and feelings of impostor phenomenon were collected from 143 participants. This was done using a combination of a self-designed questionnaire, the Iowa-Netherlands Comparison Orientation Measure (INCOM), and the Clance Impostor Phenomenon Scale (CIPS). 

After cleaning, preliminary analysis was conducted on the data to investigate descriptive statistics and correlations between variables. Afterward, the first research question was analyzed using multiple linear regression with 4 variables connected to LinkedIn usage (monthly use in hours, number of connections, number of years they have been
using LinkedIn, and whether they think their LinkedIn connections are more, less, or equally as successful as them) as predictors and the average CIPS score as the outcome variable. Statistically significant relationships with the outcome variable were found for the number of years one has been using LinkedIn (-0.81, *p*<0.05), and whether one thinks their LinkedIn connections are more, less, or equally as successful as them (8.76, *p*<0.01). However, only 15.3% of the variation in participants’ impostor scores was explained by this model.

The second research question was investigated using a mediation model with the number of years one has been using LinkedIn as the predictor, average CIPS score as the outcome variable, and the average INCOM score as the mediator. The path between LinkedIn usage and social comparison (-0.58, *p*<0.001) as well as the path between social comparison and feelings of impostor phenomenon (0.85, *p*<0.001) were statistically significant while the path between LinkedIn usage and impostor phenomenon was not significant in this model. Therefore, this model showed evidence of full mediation of the relationship between LinkedIn usage and impostor phenomenon by social comparison.

Finally, exploratory analysis was carried out to see if any of the collected demographic characteristics (age, gender, ethnicity, occupation, education) acted as moderators for the relationship between LinkedIn usage and impostor phenomenon. However, none of the moderation effects were found to be statistically significant.

Overall, the research confirmed the relationship between social comparison and feelings of impostor phenomenon previously recorded in the literature while adding a novel investigation of their relationship with LinkedIn usage. Due to the correlational nature of the data, it remains unclear whether users with lower levels of social comparison remain on the platform for longer or whether remaining on the platform for longer leads to lower levels of social comparison. Similarly, whether those who feel like impostors perceive their LinkedIn connections as more successful or whether perceiving one's LinkedIn connections as more successful leads to increased feelings of impostor phenomenon. The direction of these relationships should be the subject of further investigation within this understudied field.
